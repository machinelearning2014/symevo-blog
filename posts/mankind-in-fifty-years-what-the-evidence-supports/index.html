Mankind in Fifty Years: What the Evidence Actually Supports

November 18, 2026·17 min read

A researched look at humanity around 2076. Four aggregate trends survive scrutiny — the end of the population growth regime, longer but sicker lives, policy-contingent mass displacement, and near-total urbanisation. Four more decide how that aggregate is actually lived: daily life built on infrastructure, work transformed rather than eliminated, a technology quantified for 2030 and explicitly unquantified beyond it, and thinly measured social attitudes. Each carries a different kind of evidence, and the honest forecast keeps them apart.

Predictions about the year 2076 usually arrive in one of two flavours: a gleaming utopia of abundance, or a dust-coloured collapse. Both are cheap, because both are unfalsifiable at the point of sale. Fifty years is long enough that almost any story can be told about it, and short enough that the people writing the stories will not be around to be corrected.

So let me do something less exciting and more useful. Rather than imagine the future, I went looking for what serious, current research actually projects about the middle of this century, and — just as importantly — for how firm each projection is. That second question turned out to matter more than the numbers. The researched picture splits cleanly in two: some features of humanity in fifty years are close to structural, reported as directional trends that hold even against the threats listed alongside them. Others are explicitly scenario-branched, with the source itself saying the outcome depends on choices made in the 2020s and 2030s.

That split, not any single figure, is the real content of a fifty-year forecast. Here is what the evidence supports, trend by trend, with its confidence attached.

Part I — What happens to humanity in aggregate

1. The growth regime ends (high confidence in direction)

The single most under-appreciated fact about humanity's next half century is that it is probably the half century in which we stop growing.

The arithmetic is already in the pipeline. Global births today run at roughly 132 million a year, projected to peak around 2040–2045 at about 141 million and then drift down to about 126 million by 2100. Deaths, meanwhile, climb from roughly 63 million a year today toward about 122 million by 2100. When the two curves approach each other, the growth regime is over by construction — and demographers go further, allowing that the human population may actually begin to decline in the second half of this century if fertility keeps falling.

Two things follow, and both contradict the mental image most people carry.

First, the number of humans in 2076 is plausibly at or near its peak. A fifty-year forecast that begins with "there will be eleven billion of us" is not following the evidence; it is extrapolating a curve that has already bent.

Second, and more consequential than the headline number, the composition changes. An end to growth is an end to the young-population default. A world where deaths approach births is a world that is, on average, old — with the dependency ratios, labour-market shapes, pension arithmetic and political gravity that come with it. This is the part of the future that is least negotiable, because the people who will be old in 2076 have already been born.

I will be careful about confidence here. The direction — slowing growth, rising median age, possible decline — is reported consistently, but the central figures are genuinely contested. Population estimates today already carry uncertainty in the several-percent range, and the projections come in high, medium and low variants that diverge widely by 2076. What is robust is the shape of the transition. What is not robust is any single number with a decimal point on it.

2. Longer lives, but fewer good years (high confidence in direction, wide band on magnitude)

The second trend is the one most often told as pure triumph, and it deserves a more careful telling.

The Global Burden of Disease 2021 forecast puts global life expectancy at about 78.1 years in 2050, up roughly 4.5 years from 73.6 in 2022. Gains are largest where life expectancy has historically been lowest, so the gaps between regions are projected to narrow rather than widen.

Now the part that gets dropped. Healthy life expectancy — years lived in good health — rises only about 2.6 years over the same period, to roughly 67.4. The gap between total lifespan and healthy lifespan therefore widens. More of the added years are years lived with illness, managed rather than cured, and the disease burden shifts decisively from communicable and nutritional causes toward non-communicable ones: cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes, and the risk factors that drive them.

Two details deserve emphasis because they are where the forecast is usually misreported.

The GBD release frames the increase in life expectancy as occurring despite geopolitical, metabolic and environmental threats. That phrasing is doing real work. It is a directional claim that survives adversarial conditions, which is a stronger form of evidence than a claim that only holds in a benign scenario. It is also a claim about averages, and averages hide the distribution: a world where the mean rises while a growing share of the population spends its final decade in chronic care is not the same future as one where health span keeps pace.

And the convergence story has a floor. The report itself notes that inequalities between the highest- and lowest-income regions will remain even as the gaps shrink. Shrinking gaps are a real and underrated achievement of the last generation of global health work. They are not convergence to equality, and fifty years is not long enough to make the distinction disappear.

So the evidence-backed picture for 2076 is longer, more medically managed lives, with the burden concentrated in chronic disease and the benefits distributed more evenly than today but still unevenly. Any account of the future that says "we'll all live to a hundred" is technically adjacent to the data and substantively wrong about what those years look like.

3. Mass displacement — real, large, and genuinely undecided (low confidence in magnitude)

Here is where the confidence collapses, and where the collapse is itself the finding.

The World Bank's updated Groundswell work projects that climate change could force up to about 216 million people across six world regions to move within their own countries by 2050, with hotspots of internal climate migration able to emerge as early as 2030 and then spread and intensify. The mechanism is not dramatic: water scarcity, declining crop productivity and sea-level rise erode livelihoods and livability in exposed places, while other places offer better conditions to rebuild.

But the same report says something that changes how that number should be read. Immediate and concerted action to reduce emissions, plus support for green, inclusive and resilient development, could reduce the scale of climate migration by as much as 80 percent.

An 80 percent scenario spread is not a forecast. It is a fork in the road. Anyone quoting "216 million climate migrants by 2050" as a prediction is quoting an upper branch and discarding the branch the source itself regards as achievable. Conversely, anyone dismissing climate displacement as speculative is discarding the branch that materialises under inaction.

Two further distinctions are worth keeping straight, because conflating them produces bad policy and bad prose:

  • Internal, not cross-border. These projections concern people moving within their own countries. The politically explosive image — mass cross-border movement — is not what this research models.
  • Forced, not chosen. Migration driven by loss of livability is not the same phenomenon as voluntary mobility, and the difference matters for how receiving regions, labour markets and infrastructure should prepare.

This is the trend where the next fifty years are least written. It is also, not coincidentally, the trend where what happens in the next ten years does the most work.

4. Almost everyone will be urban (high confidence, and already mostly banked)

The fourth trend is the quietest and the most certain, because most of it has already happened.

The share of humanity living in urban areas went from about 3 percent in 1800 to about 47 percent in 2000, and about 56 percent in 2020. That is a trajectory, not a forecast, and trajectories of that kind do not reverse on a fifty-year horizon. By 2076, the overwhelming majority of humans will live in cities.

The reason this matters is not the percentage. It is the exposure. Cities concentrate everything the other three trends produce: the heat and flood risk that climate displacement responds to, the hospital capacity that aging populations consume, the housing and infrastructure stock that either absorbs or amplifies stress. A world that is old, urban and increasingly climate-stressed is a world whose resilience is decided street by street and building code by building code — not in summit communiqués.

Part II — How that aggregate is actually lived

Everything above describes humanity as a quantity: how many of us, how long we live, where we are, and whether we are still growing. All of it is compatible with almost any account of what a day is like in 2076. A person is not an aggregate. So the second half of this forecast has to answer a different question: what do ordinary people do, and what do they think about while doing it?

Here the evidence changes character, and the change is the point. Part I cited trends whose direction is reported consistently. Part II cites three quantified projections for a decade, one exposure measure that its own authors cap, one survey of one country at one moment, and a stated uncertainty that is itself the finding. These are not weaker versions of the same claim. They are different kinds of claim, and they should be labelled as such rather than inheriting the confidence of the trends above.

5. Daily living runs on infrastructure people do not see (near-term, quantified)

The most concrete thing the research says about daily life in the near future is about electricity.

The International Energy Agency projects that electricity demand from data centres worldwide is set to more than double by 2030, to around 945 terawatt-hours — slightly more than the entire electricity consumption of Japan today. AI will be the most significant driver of that increase, with demand from AI-optimised data centres projected to more than quadruple by 2030. In the United States, data centres are on course to account for almost half of the growth in electricity demand between now and 2030, and — this is the number worth sitting with — the US economy is set to consume more electricity in 2030 for processing data than for manufacturing all energy-intensive goods combined, including aluminium, steel, cement and chemicals.

The consequence for daily living is not that everyone will own a robot. It is that computation becomes ordinary infrastructure, delivered on the same terms as water and power, and competing for the same finite supply. Households already experience this category of dependency; what changes is its size. When a resource of that kind scales by a multiple in a decade, it shows up in prices, in siting decisions, in what gets built where, and in which regions get to attract anything else.

There is a measured social response to go with it, and it should be reported with its limits attached. In a Pew survey of United States adults conducted 31 July to 6 August 2023, 52 percent said they felt more concerned than excited about the increased use of artificial intelligence in daily life, against 10 percent who felt more excited than concerned. The mostly-concerned share had risen 14 percentage points since December 2022, when 38 percent held that view. Adults aged 65 and over were far more likely to be mostly concerned than mostly excited — about six in ten against 4 percent.

What that does not tell us is why the age gap exists. It is consistent with older adults being less practised with the tools, with them having more at stake in systems they already navigate, or with a general disposition of age rather than a response to a technology. The survey measures the attitude; it does not explain it. And it measures one country at one moment, which is a fact about 2023 in America, not a projection about daily life in 2076 anywhere.

6. Work is transformed, not eliminated — and the ceiling is explicit (bounded)

Employment is the domain where the evidence most invites overstatement and most clearly forbids it.

The IMF finds that almost 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI: about 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies, 40 percent in emerging markets, and 26 percent in low-income countries. It then splits the exposed jobs roughly in half — one half may benefit from AI integration, enhancing productivity, while for the other half AI applications may execute key tasks currently performed by humans, which could lower labour demand, leading to lower wages and reduced hiring. In the most extreme cases, some of those jobs disappear.

The ILO approaches the same question from the task level, scoring tasks across 436 occupations and applying them to labour-force data for more than 140 countries. It finds about a quarter of global employment falls into one of four exposure gradients: 34 percent in high-income countries, falling steadily as income declines, to just 11 percent in low-income countries. Female employment is more concentrated in the top two exposure gradients, with 5.7 percent of female employment in gradient 3 and another 4.7 percent in gradient 4.

Now the sentence that governs how every number above should be read. The ILO states that these estimates reflect potential exposure to generative AI and not the actual impact, and that they represent the upper threshold of the percentage of employment that could be affected if the technology were fully implemented. Its substantive finding is that few jobs consist of tasks fully automatable with current generative AI technology — nearly all occupations contain some tasks that require human input — and therefore that transformation of jobs is the most likely impact.

Assemble those pieces and the defensible claim is narrow. Exposure is not a forecast of unemployment. The ceiling on affected employment is roughly one quarter globally, higher in richer countries largely because adoption capacity is higher there. The ILO names the barriers to full adoption plainly: infrastructure constraints in electricity and broadband, insufficient digital skills, the cost of technology, and inherent operational difficulties. And the IMF's explanation for the lower figures in emerging and low-income economies is that those countries do not have the infrastructure or skilled workforces to harness the benefits — which is an observation about capacity, not a comforting one about safety.

Two honest caveats close this section. First, the same infrastructure gate that suppresses measured exposure in poorer countries is what would suppress the productivity benefit too; low exposure and low disruption are the same measurement, not a hedge against harm. Second, nothing here establishes the shape of employment in 2076. These are 2023–2024 assessments of which tasks a present technology could reach. A fifty-year employment forecast would require knowing what the technology becomes, which the next section says is precisely what no one knows.

7. AI is quantified for 2030 and explicitly unquantified beyond it (asymmetric — and that asymmetry is the finding)

Put the IEA figures from section 5 next to the report's own list of unknowns, and a pattern appears.

Quantified: data-centre electricity demand worldwide more than doubles to about 945 TWh by 2030; demand from AI-optimised data centres more than quadruples; US data centres take almost half of electricity-demand growth; US data processing outconsumes US energy-intensive manufacturing.

Explicitly uncertain, in the report's own words: the macroeconomic outlook, how quickly AI will be adopted, how capable and productive AI will become, how fast efficiency improvements will arrive, and whether bottlenecks in the energy sector can be resolved.

A forecast that is sharp on the physical inputs and deliberately silent on the capability is telling you something. Electricity demand is countable, contractable and subject to grid physics, so it can be projected with some precision. What the computation will do is not, and the source declines to pretend otherwise.

The IEA's own framing of the technology is worth quoting for exactly this reason: AI is a tool, potentially an incredibly powerful one, but it is up to us — our societies, governments and companies — how we use it. That is not a hedge. It is the substantive claim, and it is the same structure this post found in the climate-displacement numbers: the physical envelope is projected, and the outcome within it is decided.

So the honest answer to "what will AI be capable of in 2076?" is that the sources used here do not say, and that any confident answer is being supplied by the writer rather than the research. What can be said is narrower and more useful: the near-term footprint is large enough to reshape electricity systems, it is being built now, and the constraints that will bind it are physical ones that take years to relieve.

8. Society: the evidence is thin, dated and single-country, and should be carried as such (weakest link)

The final domain is the one where this post has the least to stand on, and saying so is part of the argument.

The only direct measurement of social attitude in the sources here is that 2023 Pew survey: one country, 11,201 adults, one week in August. It establishes that in the United States at that moment, 52 percent of adults were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life against 10 percent the reverse, that concern had risen 14 points in eight months, and that the age gap was large. All of that is real and all of it is bounded. It cannot be extrapolated to other countries, and it cannot be projected forward fifty years, because attitudes respond to events that have not happened yet.

What the economic sources add is not a social finding at all, but it is often reported as one. The ILO's gradient falls from 34 percent in high-income countries to 11 percent in low-income countries, and the IMF attributes the lower emerging-market and low-income figures to those countries lacking the infrastructure and skilled workforces to capture AI's benefits. Read carefully, that is a statement about adoption capacity, measured by the same instrument that measures exposure. It is not evidence that poorer societies will be less disrupted, less anxious, or more stable. It is evidence that they will capture less of the gain. The gap between those two readings is where most careless commentary on this topic goes wrong.

There is one more asymmetry worth naming. Every quantified claim in Parts I and II is a projection or an exposure measure produced by an institution with a model. Almost none of the social claims that will be made about the next fifty years will have that kind of backing, and they will be made with far more confidence. A reader should weight them accordingly.

What this adds up to

If you want a single sentence about humanity in fifty years, the research supports something like this: fewer of us than we expected, older than we are now, healthier for longer but sicker at the end, crowded into cities, living with whatever climate outcome this decade locks in, doing work that has been reorganised rather than abolished, and depending on infrastructure we can measure far better than we can measure what it will do.

That is a less cinematic sentence than either the utopia or the collapse. It is also the only version I can defend.

The more useful conclusion is methodological. When people ask what the future holds, they usually expect a single confidence level applied to a single story. The evidence does not support that, and the extension in Part II makes the point sharper than the original four trends could. Four separate kinds of claim now sit side by side:

  • Near-structural. The end of growth, the aging of the population, the urban majority, the direction of longevity. These follow from people already born and trajectories already established. Prepare for them; the preparation is late already in most places.
  • Scenario-branched. The scale of climate-driven displacement, which the authoritative modelling itself puts anywhere from a fifth to all of its upper projection depending on what gets done. This is not a prediction. It is a decision with a deadline.
  • Explicitly bounded. Employment exposure, which its own authors cap at about a quarter of global employment and label potential rather than actual, with transformation the stated most likely outcome. Exceeding that bound in either direction is unsupported.
  • Quantified for a decade, agnostic beyond it. Data-centre electricity demand through 2030, projected with confidence, sitting alongside an explicit refusal to project what the computation will be capable of.
  • Measured, one country, one moment. The attitude data. Real, useful for understanding 2023 in America, and incapable of carrying a claim about 2076 anywhere else.

Which means the fifty-year question is really two questions, and they have different answers:

  • What will happen to humanity regardless? Aging, urbanisation, longer lives, slower growth, work reorganised around a technology whose physical footprint is already being built. Prepare for these, because they are coming.
  • What is still being decided? How much of the century's displacement actually happens, how fast the disease burden shifts from treatable infections to chronic conditions, and what the computation is ultimately used for. These are not predictions at all. They are decisions.

The honest forecast does not tell you which future to expect. It tells you which parts of the future you are still allowed to influence — and roughly how long you have to do it.
