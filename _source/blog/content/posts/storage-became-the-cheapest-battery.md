For about fifteen years, stationary battery storage had a simple story: it rode the electric-vehicle market's cost curve. Cars paid for the gigafactories, and grid operators bought the hand-me-down economics. The interesting number was always the EV pack price, and storage was a rounding error that benefited from someone else's scale.

In 2025 that story reversed. According to BloombergNEF's annual price survey, average lithium-ion pack prices fell 8% year over year to **$108/kWh** — about **93% below 2010**. But the number that matters more is buried one line down: battery packs for **stationary storage** fell to **$70/kWh**, a **45% drop in a single year**, making stationary storage the **cheapest lithium-ion category for the first time**.

Read that again. The cheapest battery chemistry in the world is now the one that never moves.

## The crossover is the story, not the record low

Cost curves make good headlines and bad analysis, because everyone already expects them to go down. What deserves attention is the *crossing*: the moment a segment stops being a derivative of a bigger one and starts setting its own terms.

A 45% single-year drop is not a normal improvement along a learning curve. BNEF attributes it to a specific and somewhat uncomfortable combination: continued cell manufacturing overcapacity, intense competition, and an ongoing shift to lower-cost lithium iron phosphate chemistries. Note what that implies. Prices fell *despite* rising battery metal costs — lithium supply risks out of Chinese operations, and new cobalt export quotas in the Democratic Republic of Congo.

That is a striking thing for a market to do. It means the marginal cost of a stationary pack is now dominated by manufacturing throughput and chemistry choice rather than by the raw materials everyone worries about. A grid operator buying a four-hour system is no longer a price-taker on someone else's commodity exposure.

There is a second-order consequence worth naming: overcapacity-driven price declines are, by construction, not guaranteed to continue at that rate. A 45% year is a competitive shakeout showing up in the price data. Shakeouts end.

## The buildout is broadening, not just growing

Cheap cells are a necessary condition for deployment, not a sufficient one. The deployment data suggests the sufficient conditions are being met too.

The American Clean Power Association and Wood Mackenzie reported that the U.S. installed **18.9 GW** of battery storage across utility, commercial and industrial, and residential segments in 2025 — a **52% increase** over 2024. Q4 2025 alone hit **5.8 GW**. Utility-scale installations accounted for **4.9 GW** of that quarter, **31% higher** than the prior year.

But the geographic detail is the part I keep coming back to. New activity was **spread across 13 different states** — a real departure from the California-and-Texas duopoly that has historically defined utility-scale storage in the U.S.

This matters because California and Texas each have idiosyncratic reasons to lead: California's retail rate structure and net billing tariff make behind-the-meter discharge unusually lucrative, and Texas has an isolated grid with brutal scarcity pricing. Those are policy-and-market accidents as much as technological validations. When storage starts pencilling in across thirteen states with different resource mixes, different regulators, and different load shapes, the justification has shifted from *this one state made it work* to *the economics work here*.

Wood Mackenzie's outlook underlines the point: half a terawatt-hour of storage projected across 2026–2031, **250% above** the previous five-year period, with annual utility additions roughly doubling between 2025 and 2030.

## Where the honest risks live

If the technology question is broadly settled, the interesting questions move elsewhere — and it's worth being specific about which ones, because the industry's own reporting is unusually candid.

**Policy exposure is real and already biting.** Residential storage is projected to *contract* by 2% in 2026 as the Section 25D tax credit expires. Utility-scale avoided an anticipated near-term contraction only because developers rushed projects into construction during 2025 to lock in previous Investment Tax Credit requirements. That is demand pulled forward, and pulled-forward demand has to be repaid.

**Trade policy is a genuine swing factor.** Wood Mackenzie's scenarios put roughly **52 GW** in the balance. The high case — about 36 GW in 2031 — assumes permitting relief and workable Foreign Entity of Concern guidance under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which would ease domestic system and cell supply. The low case is 17% below base, assuming protectionist barriers that raise system costs and weaker load growth. Notice the asymmetry: cheap cells came substantially from overcapacity and LFP supply chains, while policy direction in several major markets leans toward raising the cost of exactly those supply chains. This is the tension worth watching, not whether lithium-ion works.

**Unit prices and system prices are diverging.** One line in the same release cuts against the whole narrative: the utility-scale battery *system* price in $/kW **increased 23% year over year**. Cells got dramatically cheaper; installed systems got more expensive. Balance-of-plant, interconnection, labor, tariffs, and financing are now the binding constraints. Anyone quoting $70/kWh as a proxy for what a project costs is quoting the wrong line of the ledger.

## What I'd watch next

Three things, in order of how much they'd change the picture:

1. **Whether the storage-per-kWh premium over general packs persists.** If stationary packs stay cheapest, the segment keeps its own supply chain and its own bargaining power. If the gap closes as EV demand recovers, storage goes back to being a follower.
2. **Interconnection and balance-of-plant costs.** The 23% system-price increase is the single most underrated number here. Cheaper cells cannot fix a queue problem.
3. **Whether the thirteen-state diffusion holds once the tax credits are gone.** Geographic spread driven by genuine local economics should survive policy expiration. Spread driven by credit timing will not. 2026 is the natural experiment.

## The takeaway

The 2025 data supports a claim that would have sounded contrarian a few years ago: battery storage has crossed from a policy-supported niche into a structural grid asset, and it did so on cost rather than on mandate. The stationary segment became the cheapest lithium-ion category on the planet while deployment spread to thirteen states — two independent indicators pointing at the same conclusion.

But "the technology won" is not the same as "the buildout is safe." The costs that fell were cell costs. The costs that rose were everything else, and the policies that accelerated 2025 demand in the U.S. are expiring or under revision. The next chapter of this story is about interconnection queues, trade regimes, and tax law — which is a much less satisfying narrative than a falling price curve, and a much more accurate one.
