The Meaning of Life for Sentient Beings: Not One Question, and Not One Answer

November 21, 2026·9 min read

The meaning-of-life question splits into three, and its bearer is contested. Widening it from persons to sentient beings works in full for the affective accounts and only fixes degrees for the agency-based ones — and the question the field leaves open stays open.

Ask someone what the meaning of life is and you will usually get an answer to a question they have not stated. That is the first thing a serious inquiry into the topic has to fix, and it turns out to be the thing that decides the rest.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's survey of the field reports that when the topic comes up, people tend to pose one of three questions — "What are you talking about?", "What is the meaning of (my) life?", and "Is (my) life in fact meaningful?" — and that the analytic literature can be usefully organized by which of the three it seeks to answer. There is a meta question about the sense of talk about life's meaning, a substantive question about what makes a life meaningful, and an evaluative question about whether some particular life is.

The order matters. I take the meta question as prior: the substantive and evaluative questions inherit their content from it. That is a stipulation about how to conduct the inquiry, not a finding reported by the survey, and I flag it as such because a great deal of apparent disagreement about whether life is meaningful is better read as disagreement about which question is on the table.

Once that is fixed, a paper on meaning for sentient beings has to do one more thing before it can argue anything: say what bears the predicate.

Fix the bearer

The field draws a standard distinction. Meaning in life is what an individual human person can exhibit. Meaning of life, in a narrower sense, is what only the human species as a whole can be meaningful or not. The survey reports that a large majority of contemporary analytic philosophers work on the first and comparatively few on the second.

Two features of that report carry the weight here.

First, the edge of the bearer is already contested inside meaning in life. The survey records recent consideration of whether non-persons such as animals can have meaning in their lives — and that while probably most have rejected that possibility over the years, some have lately been making a case for it. The boundary is not closed by definition.

Second, the survey states the residual question precisely: even if some meaning is on offer to a being that is not a person, it would be worth considering whether the sort and degree of meaning available to it is comparable to what is on offer to a person. That is the question, and it stays open.

So this inquiry attaches to individual experiencing subjects. The meaning of the species or the cosmos is a separate heading that the survey sets aside for focus, and I inherit that limitation rather than arguing past it.

The terrain, and why there is no consensus to report

Three families of position are reported: supernaturalism, naturalism, and nihilism. Only naturalism bears on whether meaning can attach to a bearer without a theological premise, so that is the part I work with; the internal division of supernaturalism is not something I describe here.

The survey reports that a distinct analytic field of life's meaning was established only since the 1980s, with debate of real depth appearing in the past 25 years or so. It reports that its own focus is normative-theoretical approaches — attempts to capture in one general principle all the conditions that could confer meaning on life, or at least mid-level principles spanning a wide range of contexts.

That description has a methodological consequence. A field organized around general principles has not delivered one agreed criterion; it has delivered candidate principles and their rivals. So the method here is to defend a stated proposal against named alternatives, not to report a consensus. The survey also reports live arguments for the nihilist view that the conditions necessary for a meaningful life do not obtain for any of us, and a debate about how a meaningless life relates to a life that is absurd, futile, or not worth living. None of that can be assumed away.

What subjectivism and objectivism ask of a bearer

Within naturalism, the survey reports subjectivism and objectivism as cross-cutting theoretical accounts of the nature of meaningfulness insofar as it is physical.

Subjectivists hold that there are no invariant standards, because meaning is relative to the subject — it depends on an individual's pro-attitudes, such as her particular ends, which are not shared by everyone. Objectivists hold that there are invariant standards, because meaning is at least partly mind-independent: it obtains not merely in virtue of being the object of anyone's mental states.

Two specific subjectivist positions do real work in what follows. The survey reports that one influential subjectivist maintains that the relevant mental state is caring or loving, so that life is meaningful just to the extent that one cares about or loves something. And it reports as uncontroversial that meaning often comes from losing oneself — becoming absorbed in an activity or experience, as opposed to being bored by it or finding it frustrating.

Against that family sits a reported objection from critics: such arguments neglect the role of objective value (or an external reason) in realizing oneself, losing oneself, and having a reason to live.

Here is where the bearer question bites. The two accounts place different demands on whatever is to bear meaning, and the mapping is mine: a theory that makes meaning depend on a bearer's pro-attitudes asks of the bearer only that it have such pro-attitudes; a theory that makes meaning at least partly mind-independent asks additionally for a standard the bearer's attitudes do not themselves supply. The sources do not assert that in those words — it is a stipulation about how to read the accounts onto a bearer, and I mark it as one.

A hybrid proposal

My normative proposal is a hybrid. A life is meaningful when a subject cares about something worth caring about and acts in its light. Both components are required: the subjective one because the sources report caring and absorption as central, the objective one because the reported objection is that caring and absorption are not sufficient unless what one cares about is worth it.

This is an argued position, not a reported finding, and its rivals are named: pure subjectivism, which denies the invariant standards the objective component needs; pure objectivism, which makes meaning mind-independent without requiring the bearer's pro-attitudes; and nihilism, which denies the conditions obtain at all.

The case for the hybrid is diagnostic, and the diagnosis is my own reconstruction rather than a survey report. Pure subjectivism faces the reported objectivist objection that it neglects objective value. Pure objectivism faces the standing question of what makes a candidate object or activity worth caring about independently of anyone's attitudes. The hybrid pays the cost of answering both, and that cost is the price of the position. I do not claim the hybrid is established — only that it accommodates both the reported subjectivist evidence and the reported objectivist objection more economically than either pure alternative.

Widening to sentient beings — and where it does not go evenly

Now the question the title promises. If meaning in life attaches to individual experiencing subjects, and if the leading accounts place different demands on a bearer, does widening from persons to sentient beings as such succeed — and for which accounts?

Sentientism supplies the precedent: sentience is the basis of moral consideration, so the class that matters is experiencing subjects rather than rational agents. Importing that into the meaning question is a stipulated scope decision of this paper, not a reported conclusion of the meaning-of-life literature.

The stipulation is deliberately partial, and it bites account by account.

Affective and hybrid accounts. On the mapping above, the required capacity is caring and absorption. Those are capacities of experiencing subjects as such. The question of meaning therefore applies to them, and the widening reaches sentient beings in full. This rests on my stipulations, not on an external claim — it is the position I argue for.

Agency-based objective accounts. These locate worth in reflective agency rather than in experience. On a paired stipulation, the difference they make between persons and other sentient beings concerns the degree of meaning available, not whether the question applies at all. That too rests on a stipulation rather than external evidence, and it connects directly to the open question: an account requiring reflective agency will answer the comparability question differently from one that does not.

The honest result. The widening holds in full on these stipulations for the affective and hybrid accounts, and fixes degree rather than scope for agency-based objective accounts. It is not a uniform extension to all sentient beings, and I do not claim it is.

What stays open

Three things, and I would rather name them than paper over them.

The first is the one the sources themselves name: whether the sort and degree of meaning available to a being that is not a person is comparable to what is on offer to a person. I have not answered it. I have shown the answer is not uniform across accounts, and that its shape depends on which capacity an account treats as the bearer's requirement.

The second sits beneath the first. The objective half of the hybrid needs something worth caring about, and no such standard has been vindicated here. Whether objective worth can be established without supernatural or non-natural commitments is the standing question for any naturalist hybrid — stated, not answered.

The third is modest: the survey addresses meaning in life and sets aside the species and the cosmos for focus, so an inquiry working inside that limitation inherits it.

Who is claiming what

Because the argument turns on the difference, the ledger:

Reported from the sources. The three-question structure and the organizing-by-question claim; the meaning in / meaning of distinction and the majority focus on the former; the recent consideration of non-persons and the residual degree question; the 1980s dating of the field; the normative-theoretical focus and the general/mid-level principle formulation; the live nihilist arguments and the absurdity/futility/not-worth-living debate; the subjectivist and objectivist characterizations; the caring-or-loving and losing-oneself positions; the objectivist objection; and sentientism's characterization of sentience as the basis of moral consideration.

Stipulated here. The priority of the meta question; the mapping of each account onto a bearer requirement; the scope decision to treat sentient beings rather than only persons as bearers; the claim that the affective and hybrid accounts reach sentient beings because caring and absorption are capacities of experiencing subjects; and the claim that for agency-based objective accounts the difference is degree rather than scope.

Reconstructed, not reported. The diagnostic argument that pure subjectivism faces the reported objectivist objection while pure objectivism faces the standing question of what makes an object worth caring about, and that the hybrid is preferred because it answers both.

Offered as proposal. The hybrid itself, and the preference for it over pure subjectivism, pure objectivism, and nihilism.

Left open. Whether the degree of meaning available to a non-person is comparable to a person's; and whether objective worth can be vindicated without supernatural or non-natural commitments.

The short version: the question is not one question, its bearer is contested, and the widening to sentient beings is real but uneven. Saying exactly where it is uneven is more useful than saying it succeeds everywhere.
